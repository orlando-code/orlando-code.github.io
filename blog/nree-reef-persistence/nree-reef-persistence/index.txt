a:T1ae5,<p>Last year I jumped at the chance to turn a data compilation task into a quantitative meta-analysis in collaboration with the lovely <a href="https://people.wgtn.ac.nz/christopher.cornwall">Chris Cornwall</a> and his students at the Victoria University of Wellington, New Zealand.</p>
<p>A few hundred papers – and many more hours – later I'm thrilled to present the results, published in <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s43017-026-00764-4"><strong>Nature Reviews Earth &#x26; Environment</strong></a>.</p>
<hr>
<p>It’s no news that coral reefs are in hot water. A recent <a href="https://www.stockholmresilience.org/research/research-stories/2025-10-13-world-reaches-first-climate-tipping-point---widespread-mortality-of-coral-reefs.html">report</a> warned that reefs have reached a tipping point from which they cannot recover.</p>
<p>If reefs are to survive, they must evolve rapidly to keep up with climate change. Our <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s43017-026-00764-4">new paper</a> examines the latest research to project what coral reefs might look like into the future. These invaluable ecosystems may yet stand a chance, but only with dramatic cuts to greenhouse gas emissions and the proliferation of heat-tolerant corals.</p>
<p><img src="/blog/nree-reef-persistence/images/FIG_S11.jpg" alt="Supplementary figure from the review illustrating reef carbonate processes."></p>
<p>Coral reefs currently provide habitat for thousands of species in tropical oceans. Their value can be crudely described in the billions of dollars they provide through fisheries, tourism, and natural coastal protection, but the true value of their ecological and cultural significance is much greater.</p>
<p>Reef structures exist because of the production of calcium carbonate, a rocky mineral, by reef-building organisms. Corals are animals which, fuelled by a delicate symbiosis with microalgae, build calcium carbonate skeletons through a process called calcification. Over time, <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms2409">these skeletons</a> and calcareous seaweeds are cemented together by <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-023-00766-w">coralline algae</a>, providing substrate for further growth or ‘accretion’. On the flip side, this calcium carbonate can <a href="https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-marine-121211-172241">dissolve in the sea water or be worn away through bio-erosion</a>.</p>
<p>The presence of a reef depends upon balancing the delicate ledger of this carbonate budget. Climate change makes ocean conditions increasingly unfavourable for calcification and better for bio-erosion and dissolution. Ocean warming brings marine heatwaves, which are the leading cause of mass coral bleaching that has <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/nature21707">led to a global decline in coral cover</a>. Ocean acidification reduces the ability of corals to build their skeletons, while deoxygenation and ocean darkening – the decrease in light reaching the seafloor due to increased sediment run-off and suspended biomass – can also reduce calcification. Together, these stressors tip the carbonate budget into the red and threaten the persistence of coral reefs.</p>
<p>What state will that budget be in under ongoing and future climate change? There is currently no scientific consensus on which organisms will build the reefs of the future, but our analysis found that coral reefs transition to net erosion over the next 50 years under all future emissions scenarios, meaning that they are eroded faster than they can grow. Yet in a future scenario in which emissions are slashed and net-zero is achieved beyond 2050, this tragic trend is reversed by the end of the century.</p>
<p><img src="/blog/nree-reef-persistence/images/animation_timeseries.gif" alt="Projected changes in reef carbonate budgets through time under future emissions scenarios."></p>
<p>Where does that leave the world’s reefs? We explore the four most likely scenarios:</p>
<p><img src="/blog/nree-reef-persistence/images/FIG_2_composite.png" alt="Composite illustration of possible future reef types and carbonate production rates."></p>
<p><strong>1.</strong> It is possible that we can already see the reefs of the future represented in present-day extreme conditions. Temperatures on some reefs have already reached as high as 39°C, with levels of acidity and oxygen-depletion similar to those expected at the end of the century. These reefs are dominated by coralline algae and slow-growing heat resistant corals.</p>
<p><strong>2.</strong> Alternatively, reefs may continue to degrade across the world. Coral cover at previously diverse sites will be decimated, and bio-eroders such as sponges and sea urchins dominate. The coral reefs we know today would cease to exist.</p>
<p><strong>3.</strong> Though <a href="https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adr2545">highly unlikely</a>, corals might naturally evolve heat tolerance that keeps pace with low to moderate greenhouse gas emissions scenarios. In this case, only about 36% of global corals would be lost, and there would be a <a href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/gcb.16647">moderate reduction in growth</a>. These heat-tolerant reefs would be dominated by faster growing corals with symbionts that can evolve heat tolerance.</p>
<p><strong>4.</strong> Widespread and sustained restoration practices encourage the proliferation of heat-tolerant corals, supported by removal of local stressors such as overfishing, sedimentation, and pollution run-off. These restored reefs would have lower biodiversity, but may facilitate the spread of these corals to further regions, and the functional persistence of reefs. However, this kind of restoration would need to cover half of global coral reefs to maintain net growth – an unlikely scenario.</p>
<p>So, where do we go from here? Despite the importance of reef systems to countries all over the world, there is a considerable lack of data in large parts of the Pacific, while studies on deoxygenation and coastal darkening are almost non-existent. Even less studied is the question of how long reef structures could persist after living corals are removed. It will take global efforts of collaboration and coordination to rapidly obtain these measurements in order to better describe the fundamental processes of reef bioerosion and dissolution.</p>
<p>The climate has been altered to the point where <a href="https://esd.copernicus.org/articles/16/275/2025/esd-16-275-2025.html">the survival of coral reefs is increasingly unlikely</a>, but their fate is not yet sealed. It is up to governments around the world, including New Zealand, to better support these initiatives before it is too late.
